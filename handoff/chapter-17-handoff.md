## 2. The chapter, beat by beat

### Beat 1 — A series that remembers (about 1.5 pages)

**Chart 1 — three AR(1) series with φ = 0.2, 0.7 and 0.95, stacked.**

*Reading:* all three are generated by the same rule with the same
noise; only the coefficient differs. At 0.2 the series looks nearly like
noise. At 0.7 it has visible runs. At 0.95 it wanders for long stretches
and looks almost like the random walk from chapter 8 — which it nearly
is, because φ = 1 is exactly a random walk. One parameter moves a
process from "essentially unpredictable" to "essentially
non-stationary", and the reader has now seen both ends of it.

**Chart 2 — the ACFs of those three.**

*Reading:* geometric decay in every case, at three speeds. The decay
rate *is* φ. This is the first time in the book that a picture and a
parameter have been the same thing, and it is worth saying so.

### Beat 2 — A series that forgets (about 1.5 pages)

**Chart 3 — an MA(1) series and an MA(2), with their ACFs. Four panels.**

*Reading:* the MA(1) ACF cuts off after lag 1 — not decays, *cuts*.
The MA(2) cuts off after lag 2. Where an AR process carries influence
forward indefinitely with diminishing weight, an MA process carries it
for exactly q periods and then stops dead.

The mechanism is worth stating plainly. In an AR process today's value
depends on yesterday's *value*, so influence propagates. In an MA
process today's value depends on yesterday's *shock*, and shocks fall
out of the window after q steps.

### Beat 3 — The identification pair (about 2.5 pages)

**Chart 4 — the four-panel grid: ACF and PACF for an AR(2) and for an
MA(1).**

*Reading, and this is the chapter's core:* the two processes are
mirror images. AR gives a decaying ACF and a PACF that cuts off at p.
MA gives an ACF that cuts off at q and a decaying PACF. That symmetry
is the Box–Jenkins identification method in one picture, and it is
genuinely usable — a reader who internalises this chart can name simple
models by eye.

**Chart 5 — the same four panels computed from real bundled data.**

Use `rec` or `soi`.

*Reading:* nothing cuts off cleanly. Both correlograms decay, both have
bumps in awkward places, and no textbook shape is present. **Say this
plainly rather than apologising for it.** The clean pictures in chart 4
came from simulated data with a known answer. Real series are mixtures,
they are contaminated, and their sample correlograms are estimates with
their own uncertainty — chapter 5's confidence bands existed for a
reason.

This honesty matters. A reader who expects chart 4 and meets chart 5 in
their own work will conclude the method is broken rather than that
their expectations were.

### Beat 4 — Both at once (about 2 pages)

**Chart 6 — an ARMA(1,1), with its ACF and PACF.**

*Reading:* neither correlogram cuts off. Both decay. Visual
identification, which worked so well two charts ago, has stopped
working — and most real series are ARMA rather than pure AR or pure MA.

That is the honest limit of eyeball identification and the reason
chapter 21 exists. Foreshadow it and move on; do not solve it here.

**Chart 7 — a parsimony demonstration: an AR(6) and an ARMA(1,1)
fitted to the same simulated ARMA(1,1) data, with both fits overlaid.**

*Reading:* the AR(6) fits about as well using six parameters where two
suffice. A pure AR model can approximate an MA component, but only by
spending parameters on it. That is what the MA term buys — the same
description, more cheaply — and cheapness is not aesthetic. Chapter 21
will show that every extra parameter costs accuracy on data the model
has not seen.

### Beat 5 — Where the process can and cannot live (about 1.5 pages)

**Chart 8 — the stationarity region for an AR(2), drawn as the
triangle in (φ₁, φ₂) space, with three example series from inside and
one from outside.**

*Reading:* not every coefficient combination gives a usable process.
Outside the triangle the series explodes, and the plotted example
should visibly do so. This is why chapter 18's optimiser cannot simply
search freely — it has to stay inside a region, and getting that
constraint right is a real implementation problem rather than a
technicality.

Mention invertibility for MA in two sentences: the same idea, the
mirror condition, and the reason software silently flips
non-invertible solutions.

### Beat 6 — Where this leaves you (half a page)

You can recognise these processes and you know what their parameters
mean. You cannot yet estimate one from data — every series in this
chapter was simulated with the answer known in advance.

## 3. The `india` box

Placed in beat 1, after chart 1.

Indian monthly inflation and industrial growth series typically show
AR coefficients in the 0.7 to 0.95 range — persistent enough that a
shock takes many months to fade, which is precisely the region chapter
9's power curve identified as hardest to test. Persistence and
testability are inversely related, and Indian macro data sits on the
awkward side of that trade.

One paragraph, connecting back to chapter 9.

## 4. What each book contributes

| Book | Take | Leave |
|---|---|---|
| **Box & Jenkins via Montgomery** | The identification method itself — the ACF/PACF signature table is theirs and remains the standard | The full iterative methodology diagram |
| **Hamilton chs. 2–3** | Stationarity and invertibility conditions done properly, and the lag-polynomial view that makes AR and MA obviously dual | The generating-function machinery |
| **Shumway & Stoffer** | Simulated examples first, real data second — the right pedagogical order, and their `rec`/`soi` examples for beat 3 | Their rapid move to estimation |
| **fpp3 §9.3–9.5** | The plain-language explanation of what an MA term *means*, which most books skip | The `fable` syntax |
| **Tsay** | That financial returns are usually close to white noise in the mean, which is why the interesting structure is in the variance | GARCH itself |
| **Cowpertwait & Metcalfe** | Simulating processes to build intuition before fitting any | R specifics |

**On examples:** `rec`, `soi`, `varve`, `GNP23` bundled. Simulated
series must be labelled as simulated — beat 3's whole point depends on
the reader knowing which is which.

## 5. Voice

**Beat 3's honesty is the chapter's most valuable half-page.** Every
textbook shows clean simulated correlograms; few say clearly that real
ones do not look like that. Say it.

**The mirror symmetry in chart 4 should feel elegant**, because it is.
This is one of the few places in the book where the mathematics is
genuinely pretty, and a sentence acknowledging that is not
self-indulgent.

Avoid, beyond earlier lists:

- Deriving the ACF of an AR(1). State the geometric decay and show it.
- The Wold decomposition theorem. It is beautiful, it is not needed,
  and it will cost a page.
- Calling MA processes "harder to understand". They are less
  intuitive, which is a different claim and a more accurate one.
