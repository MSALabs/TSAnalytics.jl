# Structural VAR

The orthogonalized impulse responses of
[Causality and Impulse Responses](12-causality-and-impulse-responses.md)
come from a Cholesky factor, and that chapter showed what it costs: the
answer depends on the order of the columns, and the order is a choice
the data cannot make. A **structural** VAR replaces the ordering with a
stated set of restrictions — economics, physics, or plain assumption —
written down where a reader can see and dispute it.

The reduced form is the VAR you already fitted. Its residuals `u_t` are
correlated, so no single one of them is "a shock to" anything. The
structural model says they are mixtures of uncorrelated structural shocks
`ε_t`:

```math
A u_t = B \varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t \sim (0, I_K)
```

so `Σ_u = A⁻¹B B′A′⁻¹`. `Σ_u` has `K(K+1)/2` distinct elements, and that
is all the information the data carry about `A` and `B` together. Every
other element has to be fixed by assumption.

    Every chart is generated by the `@example` block above it at build
    time, and each was also rendered locally and looked at before it was
    committed.

## Short-run restrictions: the A and B matrices

[`fit_svar`](@ref) takes `A` and `B` as `K × K` matrices with **`missing`**
marking the elements to estimate and numbers fixing the rest. Give only
`A` for an A-model (`B = I`), only `B` for a B-model, or both.

The running example is temperature, dew point and cloud cover at Lake
Shasta:

```@example svar
using TSAnalytics, Plots
D = dataset("climhyd")
mw = fit_var((Temp = D.Temp, DewPt = D.DewPt, CldCvr = D.CldCvr), 2)
s = fit_svar(mw; A = [1.0 0.0 0.0; missing 1.0 0.0; missing missing 1.0],
                 B = [missing 0.0 0.0; 0.0 missing 0.0; 0.0 0.0 missing])
```

That one is recursive and just-identified — six free parameters for the
six distinct elements of `Σ_u` — so it reproduces the Cholesky
responses exactly. It is the ordering of the last chapter written as an
explicit model, and the estimated `A` makes its content visible:

```@example svar
A = s.A
hm = heatmap(string.(mw.names), string.(mw.names), A; c = :balance, yflip = true,
             clims = (-1, 1), title = "Estimated A in A u = B ε", size = (520, 440))
for a in 1:3, b in 1:3
    annotate!(hm, b - 0.5, a - 0.5,
              text(s.A_mask[a, b] ? string(round(A[a, b]; digits = 3)) :
                   "$(Int(A[a, b])) (fixed)", 10, :black))
end
hm
```

Row `i` is the equation for variable `i`'s residual. The zeros above the
diagonal are the assumption: dew point and cloud cover do not move
temperature within the month. Everything below it is estimated — and
the `-0.388` says a one-unit temperature surprise comes with a `0.388`
dew-point surprise, which is what the Cholesky factor was encoding all
along.

### Over-identification is testable

Fewer free parameters than `K(K+1)/2` over-identifies the model, and
then the extra restrictions can be tested. Suppose cloud cover responds
to dew point within the month but not to temperature directly — fix
`A[3,1]` at zero:

```@example svar
s1 = fit_svar(mw; A = [1.0 0.0 0.0; missing 1.0 0.0; 0.0 missing 1.0],
                  B = [missing 0.0 0.0; 0.0 missing 0.0; 0.0 0.0 missing])
s1.lr_test
```

Rejected decisively. The estimated `A[3,1]` in the just-identified
model was small, `0.039`, but cloud cover's residual variance is small
too, and on 452 months that coefficient is very precisely not zero.
Small is not the same as absent.

More free parameters than `K(K+1)/2` cannot be identified at all, and
[`fit_svar`](@ref) refuses them before any optimisation — as `vars`
does; `statsmodels` fails later, inside, with a singular-matrix error.

!!! warning "A unit-diagonal `A` with `B = I` is over-identified"
    `A = [1 0; missing 1]` alone has **one** free parameter for a
    `2 × 2` `Σ_u`: it also forces both structural variances to one. It
    is the A-model both references' documentation usually shows, and it
    is usually rejected. The just-identified recursive model needs a
    free diagonal `B` as well, as above. `statsmodels` reports the
    reduced form's log-likelihood as its SVAR `llf` whatever the
    restrictions, which hides the problem.

## Long-run restrictions: Blanchard and Quah

Short-run zeros say what cannot happen *within the period*. Blanchard
and Quah (1989) restricted the **long run** instead, and their example is
the canonical one: output growth and unemployment, with two shocks. A
*supply* shock may change the level of output permanently; a *demand*
shock may move it for a while but not forever. That one zero, in the
long-run effect of the demand shock on the level of output, identifies
the model.

`econ5` carries the same two series for the United States:

```@example svar
e = dataset("econ5")
vb = fit_var((gnp_growth = 100 .* diff(log.(e.gnp)), unemp = e.unemp[2:end]), 2)
bq = blanchard_quah(vb)
```

[`blanchard_quah`](@ref) orders the shocks so that the restriction falls
on the first variable: the **last** shock has no permanent effect on the
**first** variable. Here that makes shock 1 the supply shock and shock 2
the demand shock. The long-run impact matrix shows the zero:

```@example svar
L = bq.lrim
hm = heatmap(["shock 1 (supply)", "shock 2 (demand)"], ["gnp_growth", "unemp"], L;
             c = :balance, clims = (-6, 6), yflip = true,
             title = "Long-run impact matrix", size = (520, 400))
for a in 1:2, b in 1:2
    annotate!(hm, b - 0.5, a - 0.5, text(string(round(L[a, b]; digits = 3)), 11,
              abs(L[a, b]) > 4 ? :white : :black))
end
hm
```

Only the first row means anything in levels: it is the permanent effect
on the level of GNP, because GNP entered in growth rates. Unemployment
entered in levels, so its row is a sum of level responses with no
long-run interpretation of its own.

The cumulative responses make the restriction visible over time:

```@example svar
plot(irf(bq, 40; cumulative = true); size = (750, 600))
```

The top-right panel is the point of the exercise: the demand shock moves
the level of GNP, and the movement dies away to exactly zero. The
supply shock's effect (top left) does not; it settles at a permanent
`0.75`.

A structural shock's sign is a normalisation, not an estimate. As
estimated, shock 2 *lowers* output growth on impact — it is a
contractionary demand shock. Multiply its column by `-1` to read it as
an expansionary one; nothing else changes.

### Structural against Cholesky

The same VAR, identified two ways:

```@example svar
ro, rs = irf(vb, 20), irf(bq, 20)
panels = []
for a in 1:2, b in 1:2
    p = plot(0:20, [M[a, b] for M in ro.orth]; color = :steelblue, lw = 2,
             label = (a, b) == (1, 1) ? "Cholesky" : "",
             title = "shock $b → $(vb.names[a])", titlefontsize = 9)
    plot!(p, 0:20, [M[a, b] for M in rs.orth]; color = :orangered, lw = 2, linestyle = :dash,
          label = (a, b) == (1, 1) ? "Blanchard-Quah" : "")
    hline!(p, [0.0]; color = :gray, label = "")
    push!(panels, p)
end
plot(panels...; layout = (2, 2), size = (750, 550))
```

Same data, same likelihood, different stories. The Cholesky ordering
says the second shock cannot move GNP growth within the quarter; the
long-run restriction lets it, and it does. Note also the bottom-left
panel: under Blanchard-Quah a favourable supply shock *raises*
unemployment slightly on impact before lowering it, where the Cholesky
ordering has it falling at once. Small, early differences of this kind
are where identification choices show.

### Which shock matters

[`fevd`](@ref) on a structural model splits the forecast error variance
by structural shock:

```@example svar
plot(fevd(bq, 20); size = (650, 500))
```

```@example svar
f = fevd(bq, 20)
println("unemployment, share from demand: h=1 ", round(f.decomp[2, 1, 2], digits = 2),
        ", h=20 ", round(f.decomp[2, 20, 2], digits = 2))
```

Demand shocks account for almost all of unemployment's short-run
forecast error and most of it even five years out. GNP growth's error is
split roughly evenly at every horizon.

## Identification is an assumption

!!! warning "The data cannot check a just-identified scheme"
    A just-identified model fits the data exactly as well as any other —
    the likelihood is the same, because every one of them reproduces
    `Σ_u` exactly. The Cholesky ordering and Blanchard-Quah above are
    both just-identified, and the data are indifferent between them.
    Which restrictions are right is economics, not statistics; only the
    over-identifying ones are testable.

That is not a defect of the method but the whole of its content. A
structural VAR is a way of making an identifying assumption explicit,
so that a reader who disagrees with it can see exactly where.

## What the structural results carry

Structural impulse responses and variance decompositions are
[`irf`](@ref)`(s, h)` and [`fevd`](@ref)`(s, h)` — the same recursion as
the reduced form's, with the structural impact matrix `P = A⁻¹B` in place
of the Cholesky factor. The result's `factorization` field records which
it was, and `plot` labels structural impulses as `shock j` rather than
with a variable's name, because a structural shock is not a variable.

`method = :scoring` (the default) is Amisano and Giannini's scoring
algorithm, as `vars::SVAR` uses; `method = :mle` maximises the same
likelihood with a quasi-Newton method. Standard errors for the free
elements of `A` and `B` come from the inverse information matrix.
